## 3. Real-World Evolutionary Biology: Sexual Reproduction {#3-real-world}

### 3.1 What Sexual Reproduction Is

Sexual reproduction is the production of genetically unique offspring through the fusion of specialized haploid cells (gametes) from two parents. It requires: (1) meiosis — a specialized cell division that halves the chromosome number from diploid (2n) to haploid (n), producing 4 gametes per event; (2) recombination — exchange of DNA between homologous chromosomes via crossing over during prophase I; (3) fertilization — fusion of two haploid gametes to restore the diploid state.

### 3.2 Mechanism: Meiosis in Detail

In humans: 46 chromosomes (23 pairs) in diploid cells. Meiosis I (reductional): homologous pairs align, cross over (average 2-3 crossovers per pair), and separate → 2 haploid cells (23 chromosomes each). Meiosis II (equational): sister chromatids separate → 4 haploid gametes. Independent assortment alone produces 2²³ ≈ 8,388,608 possible chromosome combinations per parent. With crossing over, the number of genetically distinct gametes is effectively infinite.

### 3.3 Timeline

Sexual reproduction evolved in the last eukaryotic common ancestor (LECA) approximately 2 billion years ago. First fossilized evidence from the Stenian period, ~1.05 BYA. Meiotic gene homologs found in all major eukaryotic radiations, confirming a single origin. Prokaryotes have horizontal gene transfer (conjugation, transformation, transduction) but not true meiotic sex. Nearly universal in eukaryotes; the few "ancient asexual" lineages (notably Bdelloid rotifers, ~40+ million years without confirmed sex) have alternative mechanisms (desiccation-mediated parasite escape, possible horizontal gene transfer).

### 3.4 The Twofold Cost

Maynard Smith (1971, 1978): sexual females invest ~50% of resources in sons, who cannot themselves bear offspring. An asexual mutant producing only daughters would double in frequency each generation. Experimentally confirmed: Gibson et al. (2017) measured c ≈ 2.14 in *Potamopyrgus antipodarum* (freshwater snails), consistent with and slightly exceeding the twofold prediction. Williams (1971, 1975) identified a separate "cost of meiosis" — genome dilution, where each parent passes only 50% of genes.

### 3.5 Why It Persists Despite the Cost

Multiple non-exclusive hypotheses, each with empirical support: (1) **Red Queen hypothesis** (Hamilton 1980): sex enables hosts to co-evolve with rapidly evolving parasites. Experimentally confirmed in snails, nematodes. (2) **Muller's ratchet** (Muller 1964): asexual lineages irreversibly accumulate deleterious mutations; sex purges them via recombination. (3) **Fisher-Muller effect** (Fisher 1930, Muller 1932): sex combines beneficial mutations from different individuals into one genome. (4) **DNA repair**: meiotic recombination repairs double-strand DNA breaks (~50 per cell cycle in human cells). (5) **Masking deleterious recessives**: diploidy from sex masks harmful recessive alleles.

### 3.6 Gamete Evolution: Isogamy → Anisogamy → Oogamy

The ancestral state is isogamy (equal-sized gametes, no males/females — only mating types ±). Anisogamy (unequal gametes) evolved independently at least 20 times, defining males (small gametes) and females (large gametes). Oogamy (extreme: motile sperm, immotile egg) is the condition of most animals. The twofold cost specifically applies to anisogamous species with separate sexes (gonochorism); it does not apply to isogamous species. Hermaphroditism (sequential or simultaneous) is widespread in plants, invertebrates, and some fish.
