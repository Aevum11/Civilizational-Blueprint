## Foundational Note: Why Absolute Indeterminacy Must Be Both Determinate and Indeterminate

This point has not previously been made explicit in the ET corpus, though it is a necessary consequence of the founding logic.

T is defined as Absolute Indeterminate — [0/0], the primitive that holds all resolutions open until context forces one. A first reading suggests T should be *purely* indeterminate: no structure, no determinism, nothing fixed. But this position defeats itself immediately.

**The self-referential argument:**

If T were *purely* indeterminate — exclusively, without qualification — then that would be a determined property of T. Pure indeterminacy would itself be a determination: T has been assigned the descriptor "only indeterminate." T would then be a D-constrained entity, which contradicts its foundational character. The moment you fix T as *only* free, you have made T unfree in at least one respect.

Therefore, to genuinely occupy the role of Absolute Indeterminate, T must resist even the determination of being solely indeterminate. T must be capable of being either determinate or indeterminate — and it is the *capability of being either* that constitutes the absolute. The "both" is not a compromise or a contradiction; it is the only coherent position available to something that is prior to the determinacy/indeterminacy distinction itself.

This mirrors the structure of the founding axiom exactly: *"For every exception there is an exception, except the exception."* The Exception cannot except itself without ceasing to be the exception. By the same logic, pure indeterminacy cannot be purely indeterminate without ceasing to be indeterminate. The grounded position — for T as for E — is the one that holds the opposition without collapsing into either side.

**The mathematical encoding:** [0/0] already carries this structure. It is not "undefined" (purely indeterminate, unable to take any value), nor is it any specific value (purely determinate). It is the form that simultaneously holds all resolutions open — determinate in that it is a specific form, indeterminate in that the form resolves differently in every context. The determinacy and the indeterminacy are not in tension; they are the same object seen from different descriptor vantages.

**The lattice confirmation:** T's operational manifold (U(1), the unit circle) exhibits this duality directly. The 12 discrete positions on U(1) are determinate structure — they are not T's choice, they are there. The continuous circle between those positions is T's genuine freedom — it cannot be fully specified by any finite set of descriptors. T does not choose which one of these aspects to have; both are always present simultaneously. The lattice did not impose determinism on T. The lattice reflects T's nature: a structure that is irreducibly both.

## Part III: What Is (?, ×) on the Imaginary Axis?

### 3.1 The Naive Answer — Why It Fails

The imaginary axis in the complex plane is the set {iy : y ∈ ℝ}. Under multiplication:

```
iy₁ × iy₂ = i² · y₁y₂ = −y₁y₂ ∈ ℝ
```

The product of two purely imaginary numbers is a **real number**, not an imaginary number. The purely imaginary axis is **NOT closed under multiplication**. It is not a group under ×. The question "(imaginary axis, ×)" has no group-theoretic answer in the ordinary complex plane.

This is the first sign that something deeper is happening. T's axis does not close on itself multiplicatively if you try to use it the same way as D's axis. T does not behave like D — not even algebraically.

The question resolves when stated correctly. The imaginary axis in **log₂-space** — that is, the set of purely imaginary complex numbers {it : t ∈ ℝ} used as log₂-coordinates — corresponds under the exponential map to:

```

These are complex numbers with modulus |2^(it)| = 1.
They form the unit circle: { z ∈ ℂ : |z| = 1 }
```

The imaginary axis in log₂-space exponentiates to the **unit circle** in the complex plane.

The unit circle under multiplication IS closed:

```
e^(iα) × e^(iβ) = e^(i(α+β))  →  product of two unit-circle elements = unit-circle element ✓
```
